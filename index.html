Part One

How security worked before quantum — RSA

Start with a simple question

Imagine someone hands you this challenge:

  • Here are two prime numbers. Multiply them — easy or hard?
  • Here is the answer to a multiplication. Tell me which two primes made it — easy or hard?

Everyone answers the same way: multiplying is easy, going backwards is hard. And the crucial point — computers feel exactly the same. A computer multiplies two huge numbers instantly. But handed only the result, finding the two original primes ("factoring") is brutally slow.

Now picture the numbers being 300+ digits long each. Multiplying them still takes a fraction of a second. But to "un-multiply" the ~600-digit product back into its two primes, the fastest normal computers on Earth would need longer than the age of the universe. That single gap is where internet security lived for ~50 years.

The padlock idea: public and private keys

Before RSA, all encryption was symmetric — the same key locked and unlocked, so two people had to share that key in advance (armies literally carried codebooks in locked briefcases). The unsolved problem for 2,000 years: how do you share the key over an unsafe channel without an eavesdropper grabbing it?

RSA (1977) solved it with asymmetric keys — splitting the key into two halves:

  • Public key = an open padlock. You hand it to the whole world. It can only lock.
  • Private key = the one key that opens those padlocks. You never share it.

Anyone can snap your padlock shut on a message; only you can open it. The padlock being public doesn't help an attacker — a padlock can only lock. Suddenly strangers could exchange secrets over open lines without ever meeting. That is what made the internet possible.

The actual numbers — and where the secret hides

  • Bank picks two secret primes: 61 and 53.
  • Multiplies them: 61 × 53 = 3,233. This product is made public.
  • There's a public "locking number" (e = 17) and a private "unlocking number" (d = 2,753).
Lock the secret "8": 8^17 mod 3233 = 2041 ← travels the internet Unlock it: 2041^2753 mod 3233 = 8 ← only the bank can do this

Where does d = 2,753 come from? (one layer deeper)

Think of locking and unlocking as turning a dial around a circle. Raising to the power e spins the dial part-way; raising to d spins it the rest of the way, completing the loop and landing back on the original number. For that to work, d must satisfy a condition built from the primes:

  • Subtract 1 from each prime and multiply: (61−1) × (53−1) = 60 × 52 = 3,120 — the secret "circle size."
  • Pick d so that 17 × d is one more than a whole number of loops: 17 × 2,753 = 46,801 = (15 × 3,120) + 1. That "+1" lands you back on the start.
The whole secret is this: computing d needs 3,120, which needs the two primes 61 and 53. An attacker sees only the public 3,233. To get d, they must first factor 3,233 back into its primes — the "age of the universe" problem. That is why RSA was never cracked.

Real RSA keys are typically 2,048 bits (about 617 digits) — which is why the padlock icon 🔒 in your browser has been safe for decades.

The clever part: RSA delivers a key, it doesn't lock your data

RSA math is slow, so it is not used to scramble your messages. It's used once, to deliver a small key. Then a fast cipher (AES) does the heavy lifting. This is the hybrid system every website uses.

  • Big lock (RSA) = armoured truck. Used once, carries only a tiny key.
  • Small lock (AES) = the lock on your actual data. Fast, does all the real work, and is largely quantum-safe on its own.
  • One RSA padlock per website, reused by everyone. A fresh small key per conversation, thrown away after.
Remember this split — it's the whole game later: the attacker won't bother with your data lock. They go after the armoured truck (RSA). Crack that, get the small key, and everything inside falls open.
Part Two

Quantum computing, from scratch

RSA is safe only because normal computers are too slow to factor big numbers. Quantum computers are a fundamentally different kind of machine — and on this exact problem, they are not slow.

A qubit vs. an ordinary bit

  • A normal computer thinks in bits — each is either 0 or 1. Like a coin lying flat.
  • A quantum computer uses qubits. Thanks to superposition, a qubit can be a blend of 0 and 1 at once — like a coin still spinning, not yet landed.

Why qubits get powerful so fast

  • 3 ordinary bits hold one of 8 combinations at a time. 3 qubits hold all 8 at once.
  • It doubles with every qubit: 10 → ~1,000; 20 → ~1 million; 50 → ~1 quadrillion; 300 → more than there are atoms in the universe.
  • This exponential explosion is the power: a quantum computer explores a huge ocean of possibilities together, not one at a time.

The second ingredient: entanglement

  • Superposition alone gives independent spinning coins — not useful by itself.
  • Entanglement links qubits so they share a single "fate" — measuring one instantly tells you about the other. This lets them cooperate into one real computation. (Einstein called it "spooky action at a distance.")
  • Superposition = horsepower. Entanglement = the transmission. You need both.

Why this is so hard to build

  • Measurement collapses it. Looking at a quantum state forces it to "land" on ordinary 0s/1s — the magic vanishes. Algorithms must be choreographed so the right answer survives the final measurement.
  • Qubits are absurdly fragile (decoherence). A stray vibration or hint of heat destroys the state in millionths of a second — hence the shielding chilled colder than outer space.

The golden "chandelier" myth

That famous gold object you've seen is not the computer — it's a dilution refrigerator (cooling plumbing and wiring) that keeps the chip near absolute zero. The actual processor is a tiny chip — coin-sized — dangling at the very bottom, holding all the qubits as microscopic circuits. The whole massive apparatus exists just to babysit that one twitchy chip. (Atom- and photon-based machines look completely different — more like an optics lab.)

How you add a qubit — and why it's so hard to scale

  • On the leading superconducting type, a qubit is a tiny circuit etched on a chip — like a transistor, but vastly more demanding. Adding qubits means designing a whole new chip with more circuits.
  • Each qubit needs its own control wiring, readout line, and links to neighbours — the "wiring bottleneck." Cramming more cables into a coin-sized chip near absolute zero adds heat and crosstalk. So qubits scale into the hundreds, not the billions like transistors.
  • Why not just network many cheap chips? Because chips must share entanglement to act as one computer — and entanglement can't travel an ordinary wire (you can't copy or amplify a quantum state). Linking chips needs fragile "quantum interconnects," still a major research frontier.

Physical vs. logical qubits — the number that really matters

  • A physical qubit is the raw hardware thing — real, but noisy and error-prone.
  • A logical qubit is a team of many physical qubits that constantly check each other for errors (using entanglement), acting as one reliable qubit. Like writing a number on 1,000 sticky notes so a few smudges don't matter.
  • Historically ~1,000 physical : 1 logical, now improving toward ~100:1. So a chip with hundreds of physical qubits has only a handful of reliable ones.
Every "1,000-qubit!" headline means physical qubits — fragile ones. The trustworthy (logical) count is dramatically smaller. Closing that gap is the entire race.

Can ordinary people use a quantum computer? Yes — on the cloud

  • You don't own one — you rent time over the internet (IBM, AWS Braket, Microsoft Azure, Google). Write a small program in Python (Qiskit), submit, get results in minutes.
  • Learning is essentially free (open tiers). Real machine-time is pricey: IBM ≈ $96/min; AWS ≈ $300 for a 10,000-run job on an ion machine, ≈ $5 on a superconducting one. Owning one costs millions.
  • So access is democratized — but useful quantum computing isn't yet. Today's machines still can't beat a laptop on real problems.

Where we actually stand in 2026

  • IBM has built superconducting chips past 1,000 physical qubits (Condor, 1,121), but is deliberately pivoting to smaller, better-connected, higher-quality chips — quality over raw count.
  • Google's "Willow" line (~100–1,000 physical) showed a landmark in 2024–25: adding physical qubits actually lowered the logical error rate — crossing the long-sought "break-even" threshold.
  • The field is described as moving from the noisy "NISQ" era toward early fault-tolerant computing. IBM publicly targets "verified quantum advantage" by end-2026 and ~200 logical qubits by decade's end.
Part Three

The threat & "Q-Day"

How quantum breaks RSA — Shor's algorithm

  • In 1994, Peter Shor showed a big enough quantum computer could do RSA's "impossible backward step" — factoring the public number into its two secret primes — quickly, using superposition to test the problem's structure in parallel.
  • RSA's security was always just an assumption ("factoring is hard"). Shor showed it's only hard for normal computers.
  • The catch: it needs a machine far larger than anything today — roughly ~4,000 logical qubits, which at today's overhead means 100,000–1,000,000 physical. We have hundreds. So RSA is not broken yet.

But notice the trajectory of how many qubits it's estimated to take — it keeps falling as the math improves:

Each reduction buys it by making other parts of the engineering harder — but the direction is clear, and that's what matters for planning.

The convergence toward "Q-Day"

Two lines move toward each other: machine capability rising, and the qubits needed to break RSA falling. The year they realistically meet is nicknamed Q-Day — most serious estimates land in the early-to-mid 2030s.

The reason to act NOW: "Harvest Now, Decrypt Later"

An attacker doesn't need a quantum computer today. They copy encrypted traffic now, store it, and decrypt it on Q-Day. Where does the copying happen? Not on the website — on the network path the data travels:

  • Local Wi-Fi — a fake/compromised hotspot. Small reach.
  • Your ISP — sees all your traffic. Bigger reach.
  • Backbone & undersea cables — tapping a major fibre sees millions of users' traffic at once. This is the nation-state scale, and it's documented.
  • Internet exchange points — hubs where huge traffic concentrates.

Copying ordinary data leaves no trace. So the realistic adversary already runs these taps for surveillance — to do "harvest now," they change nothing about collection; they just also keep the encrypted stuff they can't read yet.

Underwrite this point: the RSA-protected data you have already sent — and that has already been harvested — is permanently exposed the day quantum arrives. Switching to new defences cannot save it; that traffic is already sitting on an adversary's shelf, and Q-Day simply unlocks it. There are two pools — past traffic (unsavable) and future traffic (protected only after you switch). So every day still on old RSA grows the pile of secrets that can never be made safe again. That is why "migrate early" is rational, not paranoid — and why RSA will be formally retired.

Mosca's Inequality — the one formula to remember

X + Y > Z → you are exposed X = how long your migration takes Y = how long your data must stay secret Z = years until Q-Day

If migration time plus data shelf-life exceeds the time to Q-Day, your secrets are already doomed. The only lever you control is X — migrate faster. That single inequality is the entire sales logic of quantum-safe security.

The whole field, in one line: precaution is better than cure. There is no "cure" here — once Q-Day arrives, harvested data is already lost and there's no patching it after the fact. The only workable strategy is to act before the threat lands. That is the mindset the entire industry, and every government deadline below, is built on.
Part Four

The world's answer — PQC, QKD, and the NIST competition

The NIST competition

Since RSA is doomed, the world needed new math even a quantum computer can't crack. NIST ran an open, global competition to find it — deliberately public, so the whole world could attack the candidates.

  • The winners are lattice-based: Kyber → ML-KEM (FIPS 203, key exchange) and Dilithium → ML-DSA (FIPS 204, signatures). "Kyber" was the research name; "ML-KEM" is the official standard name — same recipe.
  • Because the competition was open, these algorithms are free for anyone to use — a fact that matters a lot for the business analysis later.

What is a "lattice problem"?

  • RSA's hard problem was factoring; Shor kills it. So a brand-new hard problem was needed — one Shor's trick doesn't apply to.
  • A lattice is a giant grid of points — but in hundreds or thousands of dimensions (videos show 2D/3D only because that's all you can draw).
  • The puzzle: given a point slightly off the grid, find the nearest real grid point. Trivial in 3D; impossible-to-shortcut in 800 dimensions — even for a quantum computer. That's why lattices are quantum-resistant.
  • Keys are still a public/private pair: the public key is a scrambled description of the grid; the private key is the secret shortcut to the nearest point.

This is not theoretical — it's already running on billions of devices

The most important proof that the switch can really be done: the world's most security-conscious companies have already done it.

  • Signal (2023, "PQXDH") and Apple iMessage (2024, "PQ3") already deployed post-quantum key exchange to billions of users — both using Kyber/ML-KEM, both built in-house from the free NIST standard, and independently verified by academic cryptographers. Major browsers and Cloudflare have done the same. You are very likely using post-quantum encryption already, without noticing.
  • No vendor sold them this. The PQC algorithm is free and open — anyone capable can implement it. (This is the key point for valuing software-only PQC products: the math is a commodity; the moat is elsewhere.)
  • During transition, systems run hybrid (old RSA + new PQC together) as a safety net — then drop RSA entirely.
Put the pieces together and the transition is clearly real and underway: the algorithm exists and is free, it is already proven at planet scale (Apple, Signal, browsers), and it is now mandated by government deadlines (next section). The question is no longer "will this happen" but "who supplies it, and how fast each organisation moves."

Key insight: on a QKD link, RSA becomes redundant — QKD is the secure channel RSA was only ever a workaround for. And for everywhere without QKD fibre, PQC replaces RSA.

Why randomness is the hidden foundation

  • A key is a long string of 0s and 1s. If it's predictable, every lock is useless (an unbreakable vault with your birthday as the combination).
  • Normal computers can't be truly random — they use a formula (pseudo-random), guessable if you learn the formula or seed.
  • A QRNG uses physics — genuinely unpredictable. Every algorithm (RSA, AES, PQC, QKD) consumes randomness whenever it makes a key. It's the floor under the whole building — and useful today, not just after Q-Day.

The distinctions people trip on

  • Tropos vs Armos: Tropos makes a random number in one place (dice machine). Armos delivers a key between two distant sites (uncopyable courier). Generation vs distribution. (Armos uses Tropos for its randomness.)
  • Hodos vs Armos: not stacked — siblings. Two ways to do the same job: math over normal internet vs physics over special fibre.
  • Why Armos if Hodos already beats quantum? Hodos is safe by assumption; Armos is safe by physics and detects eavesdroppers. Crown jewels want the guarantee, not the bet.

The fibre reality (why QKD stays niche)

  • QKD uses ordinary optical fibre — often spare "dark fibre" — deployed underground between fixed sites.
  • It does not work over undersea cables: those amplify the signal every ~80–100 km, and amplifying a photon destroys it (no-cloning). That caps each link at ~150–200 km.
  • To go further over land → chain "trusted nodes" (each a point you must physically secure). To cross oceans → satellites (China's Micius), not cables.
